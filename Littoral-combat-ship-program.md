# Littoral Combat Ship Program

The Littoral Combat Ship (LCS) program was built around an ambitious operating concept: fast, comparatively small surface combatants with extensive automation, reduced crews, and modular mission packages that could be changed as missions changed. By 2022, however, both LCS variants were facing serious technical and operational problems that weakened those assumptions.

The Freedom class suffered repeated combining-gear failures in the propulsion system, while the Independence class developed structural cracking in high-stress areas of the hull and superstructure. At the same time, the modular mission concept had been reduced from its original ambition, operating and maintenance costs were higher than expected, and the Navy was considering early retirement of multiple relatively young Freedom-class ships.

## Results

The War Zone reported in March 2022 that the Navy was considering retiring eight to ten Freedom-class ships as part of the FY2023 budget proposal, even though many of the ships were still relatively young. The article linked the proposal to the class-wide combining-gear problem and noted that the corrective modification was extensive enough to require major hull work.

Navy Times reported in May 2022 that structural cracking had been identified across the Independence class. Six of thirteen ships had experienced cracks, and internal records showed operational restrictions intended to limit crack propagation. For USS Omaha, those restrictions included limits above 15 knots in sea state 4 and higher sea states. The Navy and shipbuilder were implementing structural modifications, including thicker deck and shell plate in affected areas.

The broader program challenge went beyond a single defect. The original concept depended on speed, automation, a small crew, maintainability, and modular mission capability working together. When propulsion reliability, structural durability, mission-package availability, and maintenance burden did not mature as expected, the operating model itself became harder to sustain.

## Transformation analysis

Through the strategic design lens, the LCS program was based on an ambitious operating model where ships were expected to be fast, flexible, highly automated, and capable of performing different missions through specialized mission packages. Simple tasks should have been automated, leading to traditional roles being eliminated and needing fewer people for the repetitive tasks and more people to fulfil the new roles that should concentrate on integration and problem-solving. Theoretically, this would allow a small and flexible crew to operate a technologically advanced ship; however, in practice, it created a strong dependence on the reliability of both the ship and its automated systems. This meant that when technical problems appeared, the basic assumptions behind the design became harder to sustain.

The Freedom-class ships experienced repeated failures because of a design flaw in the combining gear. This component allowed the ships to reach the high speeds the Navy needed as a central requirement. As the repairs took a long time and were costly, the Navy eventually faced the possibility that repairing these ships would not justify the cost. A different but equally important design problem happened with the Independence-class ships. In these cases, Navy documents described parts of the ship as having under-designed structural defects that led to hull and superstructure cracks, and some of the ships were restricted to speeds below 15 knots in certain sea conditions. This was a serious contradiction for a ship originally designed around speed and agility. With these problems and restrictions, the Navy had to abandon the idea of rapidly exchanging mission packages. Some specialized packages were delayed, and the ships became more expensive and complicated to maintain than originally anticipated.

On the cultural lens side, the program involved a significant organizational transformation in addition to the new design. The system was designed around smaller crews and extensive automation, leaving little room for weaknesses in maintainability, system integration, or automation reliability. In this case, they were still in the transformational process when the design problems began. We can deduce that this eventually led to a distrust in the technology and made the decision-makers more certain that the ships must be decommissioned before the losses were greater.

The political lens is less directly documented in the case, but it helps explain the difficulty of changing course. The Navy had made substantial procurement and production commitments around the LCS concept. Once defects appeared across ships already built or underway, decisions about repair, continued operation, and early retirement involved more than engineering. They also required balancing prior investments, institutional expectations, and future fleet needs. This is a supporting interpretation, not a change to the original analysis above.

It can be deduced that the strategic design lens was the most influencial one for the decommissioning of the ships, because the design of the product got constant failures and didn't met the specifications. The cultural and political lens also had its contributions, but with less impact than the strategic design one.

## Lessons

- Test the operating model, not only individual technologies. Structural durability, propulsion, automation, maintenance workload, crew assumptions, and mission systems need to work together under realistic conditions.

- Use staged commitments and decision gates. Critical capabilities should be demonstrated before scaling production to a point where corrective action becomes class-wide and expensive.

- Treat maintainability and lifecycle cost as design requirements. Acquisition cost alone does not describe whether a technology-intensive platform will remain economical to operate.

- Design for degraded modes. Automation can reduce routine workload, but crews still need clear procedures, training, and sufficient capability when automated or mechanical systems fail.

- Keep the intended business or mission outcome visible. Technical sophistication is not a substitute for operational capability.

## Conclusion

The LCS case shows the cost of scaling an ambitious future-state design before every dependency has matured. Transformation programs often combine new technology, new roles, new processes, and new performance expectations. When those elements are tightly coupled, leaders need stronger validation gates, lifecycle thinking, and contingency planning than they would for a conventional technology upgrade.
