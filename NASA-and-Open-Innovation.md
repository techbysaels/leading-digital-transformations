# NASA and Open Innovation

NASA's Space Life Sciences Directorate (SLSD) turned to open innovation after a major budget reduction forced the organization to reconsider how it sourced ideas and solved research problems. The pilots were technically compelling as external solver communities produced useful solutions to difficult problems, including a substantial improvement in solar-event prediction and an optimization algorithm later incorporated into NASA's medical-kit planning model. Yet the January 2011 workshop revealed a different problem. Evidence that the method worked did not automatically translate into organizational adoption.

This case is valuable for digital-transformation leaders because it separates proof of technical value from acceptance of a new operating model. The central challenge was not whether open innovation could generate results; it was whether experts whose professional identity was built around being NASA's problem solvers were prepared to redefine their role as people who could also frame problems, search externally, evaluate outside solutions, and integrate them into mission work.

## Proposed solution

Jeff Davis and Elizabeth Richard did not introduce open innovation as a single software tool. They developed it gradually as a new research mechanism. The effort included benchmarking other organizations, lectures and workshops, a market assessment of open-innovation providers, challenge-owner training, and pilots using InnoCentive, yet2.com, TopCoder, and the internal NASA@work platform.

The design was attractive under tighter budgets because it expanded the pool of potential problem solvers without requiring NASA to build every capability internally. It also required a different discipline: research problems had to be translated into challenge statements, evaluation criteria had to be defined in advance, and internal experts had to be ready to assess ideas developed outside their normal networks.

## Results

- More than 2,800 solvers from over 80 countries participated across the InnoCentive and yet2.com challenges.
- A winning solar-event solution delivered predictions within eight hours at 85% accuracy with a three-sigma confidence interval, materially improving on the capability NASA had before the challenge.
- The TopCoder medical-kit challenge produced an optimization algorithm that NASA incorporated into the Integrated Medical Model used to support future space-travel medical-kit design.
- Other challenges generated new leads, candidate materials, and approaches that NASA had not identified through its traditional internal process.

## Adoption failure

The January 2011 workshop exposed resistance that was deeper than a lack of information. Some researchers questioned the confidentiality and completeness of external challenge platforms. Others saw the approach as inconsistent with the scientific method they had been trained to follow. The strongest concern was professional identity: asking outsiders for solutions could feel like admitting that NASA's own experts did not know the answer.

This created a classic transformation gap. Leadership had invested heavily in demonstrating that the new method could work, but many potential users were evaluating a different question about what would this way of working mean for their expertise, autonomy, status, and daily research practice.

## Transformation analysis

From a strategic design perspective, the shift made sense. After the 45% R&D budget reduction in 2005, NASA’s Space Life Sciences Directorate still needed to solve difficult research problems, but it had fewer resources to rely on. Open innovation offered a way to reach expertise outside the organization rather than trying to build every capability internally. The pilots showed that this could work: external solvers improved solar-event prediction and developed an algorithm that NASA later used in medical-kit planning. Still, the strategy required more than posting challenges online. Researchers needed to define problems carefully, establish evaluation criteria, and work out how outside solutions would become part of NASA’s own processes.

The cultural lens best explains why the successful pilots did not immediately lead to wider adoption. NASA researchers were used to being the people who found the answers. Inviting outsiders into that process could feel like questioning their expertise, even when the results were promising. At the January 2011 workshop, concerns about confidentiality, scientific rigor, and professional identity made that tension visible. In that sense, the challenge was not simply to persuade people that the technology worked. It was to help them see that asking a good question, judging external ideas, and bringing a solution into mission work were also valuable forms of expertise.

The political lens also played a role, although the case offers less direct evidence about formal power struggles. A new research model changes who can propose a solution, whose judgment carries weight, and who has responsibility for approving and using the result. Those shifts can create understandable concerns about control, autonomy, and accountability. Jeff Davis and Elizabeth Richard responded by building support through workshops, training, provider assessments, and pilots rather than treating open innovation as a mandate. For broader adoption, they would need credible internal champions and clear rules for deciding which problems belonged on external platforms and how resulting solutions would be evaluated.

It can be deduced that the cultural lens was the most influencial one for the failure of the proposal because it helps explain why, by the January 2011 workshop, strong results had not yet made the approach routine. A key lesson is to work on adoption alongside the technical validation, as the solution could be the best one possible, but it will not work if no one wants to use or develop it. Some strategies to apply could be to involve the researchers in shaping the challenges, more clarification on confidentiality and decision rights, or to demonstrate the value of the method through colleagues the researchers trust.

## Lessons

- Pilot success is evidence, not adoption. A technically successful proof of concept still needs a deliberate plan for changing routines, roles, incentives, and decision rights.

- Professional identity can be a material implementation constraint. When a new model changes what it means to be an expert, leaders need to address that explicitly.

- The operating model must be designed with the technology. Open innovation required new capabilities in problem framing, external search, evaluation, IP/confidentiality management, and solution integration.

- Early adopters matter, but broad adoption requires credible peer examples inside the organization. External analogies were often less persuasive to NASA scientists than mission-relevant evidence.

- Transformation communication should explain when the new method is appropriate and when it is not. Treating open innovation as another tool in the research toolkit is more credible than positioning it as a universal answer.

## Conclusion

For a leader managing digital transformation, the NASA case is a reminder that implementation risk often sits outside the technology. Strategy created the need for a more open innovation model, the pilots validated that it could create value, and culture determined whether the organization was willing to use it. The leadership task was therefore to connect all three: a compelling reason to change, a workable process, and a professional environment in which experts could adopt the new process without feeling that their expertise had been devalued.
